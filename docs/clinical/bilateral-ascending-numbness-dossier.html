Urgent safety assessment — read first

This needs medical assessment within days, not at the next routine appointment

Three things changed at once: the numbness crossed above the knees for the first time, it began appearing while walking rather than only while standing still, and she reports that her legs are not supporting her. That is a decompensating picture, not a stable one. A one-year history must not be allowed to make it look reassuring.

All seven reviews agreed this is urgent but not, on the information available, an emergency. What converts it to an emergency is the list below.

Emergency criteria

Go to an emergency department today if any of these appear

These are not hypothetical. In a patient with a bilateral, progressive, ascending deficit, each one signals a lesion where the delay before treatment determines whether the damage becomes permanent.

Same-day emergency — do not wait for an appointment
  • Any new bladder or bowel change — inability to pass urine, leaking, losing the sensation of needing to go, or new faecal incontinence. This is the single most important red flag.
  • Numbness of the saddle area — the groin, genitals, inner thighs or the area you sit on.
  • New or rapidly worsening leg weakness — foot dragging, knees buckling, inability to rise from a chair without using her arms.
  • A band of altered sensation around the trunk — chest or abdomen. This indicates the spinal cord rather than the nerves.
  • Severe back pain that wakes her at night, or back pain with fever.
  • A suddenly cold, pale or mottled, painful foot or leg — acute loss of blood supply; irreversible damage occurs within hours.
  • Any foot wound, blister, black area or ulcer that is not healing — however small, and especially if painless.
  • A fall with a head strike, particularly if she takes a blood thinner.
  • New confusion, or symptoms of severe low blood sugar.

Put this list on the fridge. The family should know it without looking it up.

Section 01

Executive summary

Seven specialist reviews were run independently and then cross-examined against each other. They converged on one formulation and disagreed productively about its weighting.

The consensus formulation

This is most likely not one disease. It is a background length-dependent polyneuropathy (diabetes is the obvious candidate but not the only one, and possibly not the main one) plus a second, posture-dependent mechanism layered on top — most plausibly lumbar spinal narrowing, a blood-pressure drop on standing, or both — plus a third, separate problem of muscle loss that is being reported as part of the same symptom but is not neurological at all.

Three distinct problems, three different treatments, three different specialists. Treating only the diabetes will fail.

The four findings that should change what happens next

  1. There are reversible causes that have probably not been excluded. Vitamin B12 deficiency produces this exact syndrome — symmetric, ascending, large-fibre sensory loss with the sensation of not feeling the ground. The endocrinology review judged it a better fit to her described phenotype than diabetic neuropathy. A normal serum B12 does not exclude it; methylmalonic acid is required. If her thyroid disease is Hashimoto's, her risk is higher still.
  2. An over-the-counter supplement may be causing or worsening it. High-dose vitamin B6 causes a sensory neuropathy that mimics this presentation and is reversible on stopping. The products that contain it are marketed for “nerve support” and “tingling feet” — meaning a family can cause this while trying to treat it. Two independent reviews ranked auditing this as a top action.
  3. Her symptoms may be worsening because her diabetes control improved. Rapid falls in HbA1c can trigger an acute iatrogenic neuropathy. This reframes “no improvement despite treatment” as possibly “worse because of the speed of treatment” — and it is testable from records that already exist.
  4. The best-evidenced treatment available to her is not a drug. Supervised balance and strength exercise reduces falls by roughly a quarter on high-certainty evidence from nearly 13,000 people. No medication, supplement or device in this dossier comes close to that. Her chief complaints — losing the ground under her feet, legs not holding her — are balance and strength outcomes, not pain outcomes.

What is realistically achievable

Stated plainly, because the distinction governs every decision below. Lost sensation is the hardest thing to recover and may not return. If a deficiency or a toxic supplement is found and corrected early, meaningful sensory recovery is genuinely possible. If this is established diabetic nerve damage, the realistic goal is to stop the progression, not reverse it. But walking, standing tolerance, stability, confidence and independence can all improve substantially even if the numbness never does — and that is where the strongest evidence sits.

Section 02

Most likely explanations, ranked

  1. Length-dependent polyneuropathy — present in almost any reading very high

    Toes first, symmetric, ascending as a graded stocking. Diabetes is the default explanation, but the tempo is wrong for it: reaching the knees in about twelve months is faster than typical diabetic neuropathy, which usually takes five to fifteen years. That tempo is itself the argument for looking harder at B12, thiamine, B6 excess, paraproteins and inflammatory causes.

  2. A second, posture-dependent mechanism on top — spinal, haemodynamic, or both high

    Required to explain why standing provokes it. The candidates are lumbar canal narrowing (relieved by bending forward) and a blood-pressure drop on standing (relieved within seconds of sitting). These are distinguished at the bedside, not by argument.

  3. Sarcopenia and lean-mass loss — explaining “my legs won't support me” high

    A quarter or more of weight lost on a GLP-1 is lean tissue. At 80, that can cross a disability threshold. This does not cause numbness, but it plausibly causes a large share of the instability — and it is measurable and treatable. Sensory failure is worse in the dark; strength failure shows up rising from a chair.

  4. Vitamin B12 deficiency, including metformin- and PPI-related high

    Fits the large-fibre, proprioceptive phenotype better than diabetes does. Cheap, treatable, and progressive if missed. Requires B12 with methylmalonic acid.

  5. Vitamin B6 toxicity from supplements moderate if any supplement exposure

    Conditional: negligible if she takes no supplements, a leading candidate if she takes a “nerve support” product. It is the one item on this list that could be a complete and reversible explanation, and it is free to check.

  6. Thyroid dysregulation — over-replacement more than under moderate

    Weight loss lowers the levothyroxine requirement while the dose stays fixed. Over-replacement causes atrial fibrillation, bone loss and muscle weakness — which would worsen exactly her presenting complaint.

  7. Other medication and metabolic contributors moderate

    Orthostatic drugs, amlodipine-related foot swelling altering sensation, kidney function overestimated by creatinine, electrolyte disturbance, and — if present — amiodarone or long-term nitrofurantoin, which are genuine causes of neuropathy.

  8. Paraproteinaemia / monoclonal gammopathy moderate

    Prevalence rises steeply past 70. Named by the AAN as one of only three highest-yield tests in this presentation. Requires immunofixation, not protein electrophoresis alone.

  9. Treatment-induced neuropathy of diabetes low probability, high test value

    Mechanistically compelling and highly relevant to the GLP-1, but two reviewers argued against it as the primary explanation: classical cases are acute and severely painful with autonomic features, whereas her course is gradual and her complaint is numbness. Low probability — but uniquely worth testing, because it costs nothing but pulling the HbA1c record, and if positive it changes how fast her diabetes should be treated.

  10. Kidney disease, thiamine, folate, copper, vitamin E, alcohol low to moderate

    Individually unlikely, collectively worth one blood draw. Alcohol must be asked about directly and non-judgementally — it produces a near-identical picture and is among the most commonly missed contributors at this age.

  11. CIDP, cord compression, myelopathy, dural AV fistula low

    Low probability, high consequence, and all treatable. They are why the examination in section 14 matters and why imaging should not be narrowly scoped. See section 03.

  12. Peripheral arterial disease as the cause low

    Roughly 3–5%. Standing-still provocation is the wrong trigger for arterial disease, which needs muscular work. But see section 11: it is likely enough to be present that it must still be excluded, for entirely separate reasons.

The anchoring trap

The most likely diagnostic error in this case is “she is diabetic, therefore this is diabetic neuropathy.” The ADA's own Standards of Care state that diabetic neuropathy is a diagnosis of exclusion, and that non-diabetic neuropathies occur in people with diabetes and may be treatable. In the population-based Rochester study, about 10% of neurological deficit in people with diabetes was attributable to non-diabetic causes — and her atypical features mean that figure understates the risk here.

Section 04

Why standing triggers it — the most informative feature in the whole case

This is the detail that should drive the consultation, because it is the one finding that ordinary diabetic nerve damage cannot easily explain.

Diabetic neuropathy is structural, fixed damage to the longest nerve fibres. It is therefore constant — classically worse at night and at rest. It does not switch on after five minutes of standing and switch off when she sits down. A symptom governed by posture requires a mechanism that changes with posture, and there are only three:

Where the specialists disagreed — and why it matters

The neurologist initially argued that no metabolic diagnosis can explain posture dependence, making spinal stenosis the active driver. The spine specialist pushed back: small-fibre and early large-fibre neuropathies are commonly provoked by prolonged weight-bearing, so the standing trigger is suggestive but not specific. That disagreement is unresolved on the current information — and it is resolved not by argument but by three bedside tests in section 14.

The one question worth more than most tests

Ask her precisely this, and write down the answer: “When it comes on while you are standing, what makes it go away — sitting down, leaning forward onto something, or just stopping and staying upright?”

Relief by bending forward → points to the spine. Relief within seconds of sitting, with light-headedness → points to blood pressure. Relief from simply standing still after walking → points to arteries. The three answers send the investigation in three different directions, and the distinction is free.

Section 05

Why crossing above the knee matters

It matters because it distinguishes between three quite different situations, and because it is the objective evidence that this is progressing rather than static.

  1. A neuropathy advancing up the leg. Nerve fibres die back from the tip, so the numb zone climbs as a graded stocking with no sharp upper border. Reaching the knees in roughly twelve months is faster than typical diabetic neuropathy, which usually takes five to fifteen years — and that tempo is itself a reason to look for an additional cause.
  2. Higher nerve roots being recruited. As spinal narrowing worsens, the roots supplying the thigh (L2–L4) join those supplying the foot. This tracks worsening compression.
  3. A true sensory level — the concerning one. If the numbness has a sharp upper edge that can be mapped as a line, and especially if it extends onto the trunk, the problem is the spinal cord itself, not the leg nerves. This changes both the urgency and which part of the spine must be scanned.
Do this at home tonight, and again in a month

With a soft pencil or washable marker, and something lightly scratchy (a folded paper edge), map the upper border of reduced sensation on each leg and measure it in centimetres from the kneecap. Write the date. Repeat monthly.

This costs nothing and creates the objective progression record that no clinic has. “The border rose 9 cm in six weeks” is a fact a neurologist must act on; “it seems to be getting worse” is not. Also note whether the border is gradual or sharp — a sharp, mappable line is a finding to report promptly.

The cascade nobody checks

GLP-1 → dehydration → kidney injury → drug accumulation

Nausea, vomiting or poor fluid intake cause volume depletion, which causes pre-renal kidney injury. A fall in kidney function then raises blood levels of metformin (lactic acidosis), gabapentin and pregabalin (sedation, ataxia, falls — precisely the drugs likely to be added for her numbness), digoxin, SGLT2 inhibitors and anticoagulants. The result looks neurological — worsening unsteadiness, heavy legs, confusion — when it is drug accumulation. Ask the prescriber for sick-day rules and a plan for checking kidney function during dose escalation.

De-prescribing priorities — ranked, with the question to ask

  1. Any B6-containing supplement or “nerve support” product

    “Exactly how much pyridoxine am I getting per day from all products combined, and should it stop?” The only item that might be a complete explanation.

  2. Metformin

    “Can we check methylmalonic acid, not just B12? Should I take B12 supplementation regardless?” Do not stop metformin — supplement B12.

  3. Sulfonylurea or insulin alongside the GLP-1

    “Were these reduced when the GLP-1 started, and again as my weight fell? What is my hypoglycaemia risk?” Low blood sugar at 80 presents as unsteadiness and falls, not sweating — and a beta-blocker masks it further.

  4. The whole antihypertensive load, especially alpha-blockers and nitrates

    “Can we do lying-and-standing BP before deciding? Is my target still right at 80 with neuropathy and fall risk?” Weight loss predictably lowers the requirement — doses right at her old weight are now excessive.

  5. Amlodipine

    “Could this be causing my foot swelling and the heavy, wooden feeling? Is a lower dose or alternative reasonable?”

  6. Beta-blocker

    “Is there still a clear indication? It blunts my ability to defend blood pressure on standing and masks low blood sugar.” Never stop abruptly.

  7. Any PPI, sedative, benzodiazepine, Z-drug, opioid or anticholinergic — including over-the-counter sleep and allergy products

    Cumulative anticholinergic and sedative burden drives falls and confusion. Taper, never stop abruptly.

  8. Levothyroxine dose and timing

    “Can we check TSH and free T4? Should the dose change given my weight loss, and am I timing it correctly around the GLP-1, calcium, iron and any PPI?”

  9. Things possibly missing

    Vitamin D, B12 if deficient, thiamine if on a long-term loop diuretic, adequate protein, bone protection — plus the non-drug essentials: podiatry, a formal falls assessment, physiotherapy, a home hazard review and a vision check.

Section 08

Diabetes-specific analysis

Diabetic neuropathy is almost certainly part of this picture. The question is whether it is being allowed to explain too much.

How well it actually fits

Supports diabetic neuropathyArgues against it as the whole story
Symmetric, distal, toes-first, length-dependent, ascending. Loss of large-fibre and proprioceptive function. Diabetes present. Obesity and raised triglycerides are independent neuropathy drivers in their own right. Posture dependence — diabetic neuropathy is constant, classically worse at night and at rest, not switched on by standing. Tempo — toes to above the knees in 12 months, against a typical 5–15 years. “Legs won't support her” — a strength and proprioception statement that pure neuropathy rarely produces this fast.
The cheap test of whether diabetes is the culprit at all

Neuropathy, retinopathy and nephropathy share the same microvascular substrate and travel together. Request the dilated retinal exam report and a urine albumin:creatinine ratio. Documented retinopathy or albuminuria makes microvascular diabetic neuropathy substantially more probable. Their absence — especially if her diabetes duration is short — weakens the diabetic attribution considerably and should redirect effort toward B12, B6, paraproteins and the spine.

Glycaemic control: what it can and cannot do

This is the most important place not to overclaim. The Cochrane review of enhanced glucose control for diabetic neuropathy found high-quality evidence of benefit in type 1 diabetes — but in type 2, the reduction in clinical neuropathy did not reach statistical significance. The large trials (ACCORD, ADVANCE, VADT) did not demonstrate meaningful neuropathy benefit from intensification, and ACCORD showed excess mortality in the intensive arm.

Therefore

Driving her HbA1c down is not a credible treatment for her neuropathy, and at 80 it carries real harm. The ADA target for an older adult with complex health and functional impairment is under 8.0%, with avoidance of hypoglycaemia explicitly prioritised over numerical tightness. If health status is judged poor, the ADA position is to drop the numerical target entirely and simply avoid hypoglycaemia and symptomatic hyperglycaemia. An HbA1c of 6.5% in this woman is a red flag, not an achievement — and warrants a de-intensification review.

Treatment-induced neuropathy of diabetes — the paradox worth testing

Rapid correction of long-standing high glucose can trigger an acute neuropathy. The mechanism is thought to be an energy crisis in the small nerve fibres and their microvasculature — the same phenomenon as the well-known early worsening of retinopathy after rapid glycaemic correction, in a different tissue.

A disagreement between reviewers, stated honestly

The risk rises steeply with the size of the HbA1c fall over three months — that direction is agreed and is the actionable point. But two reviewers extracted different figures from the same paper: one read roughly 20% risk at a 2–3 point fall and over 80% above 4 points; the other read roughly 10% at 2 points, 50% at 4 points and over 90% at 5 points. Both agree about 11% of the studied cohort met the criteria. The treating team should read the source directly rather than rely on either figure.

Two reviewers also argued this does not fit her. Classical cases are acute and severely painful with prominent autonomic features; her course is gradual over a year and her complaint is numbness. So it is a low-probability hypothesis — but uniquely worth testing, because it costs only the retrieval of existing records, and a positive answer changes how fast her diabetes should be treated from here.

What to bring: every HbA1c with its date, the GLP-1 start date and every escalation, and weights with dates. Plot symptom worsening against the curve. A fall of more than 2 points with symptom escalation inside about 8 weeks supports it; a stable HbA1c, or symptoms clearly predating the GLP-1, largely refutes it.

Section 09

Thyroid-specific analysis

Hypothyroid neuropathy is real but requires overt, untreated hypothyroidism, and is typically mild. She is on replacement. As a primary explanation: low. But the thyroid matters here for two other reasons, one of which may be the most useful clue in the case.

The over-replacement trap

Levothyroxine dosing is weight-based. Successful weight loss lowers her requirement while the dose stays fixed — and the GLP-1's delayed gastric emptying additionally alters how the drug is absorbed. The result drifts toward over-replacement, which in an elderly cardiac patient means atrial fibrillation and stroke risk, accelerated bone loss and fracture, and a myopathy causing proximal weakness — which would worsen exactly her presenting complaint.

  • Check TSH and free T4, not TSH alone. They can dissociate: a normal TSH with a high free T4 still indicates tissue over-exposure.
  • Use an age-adjusted range. TSH rises physiologically with age; reported upper limits reach roughly 6–7 mIU/L in the over-70s. A TSH of 5–6 at 80 may be entirely normal and does not by itself justify increasing her dose. Aim for the upper half of the age-adjusted range, not the lower.
  • Recheck 6–8 weeks after every GLP-1 escalation and every 5–10% of body weight lost.
  • Fixed timing — fasting, 30–60 minutes before food, and at least 4 hours from calcium, iron and magnesium; PPIs also reduce absorption.
The genuinely useful clue — ask what kind of thyroid disease she has

If it is Hashimoto's (autoimmune), she is at increased risk of other autoimmune conditions — notably autoimmune atrophic gastritis and pernicious anaemia, which cause B12 deficiency. And B12 deficiency produces precisely her syndrome: symmetric, ascending, large-fibre sensory loss with lost position sense, unsteadiness, and the feeling of not sensing the ground. The endocrinology reviewer's assessment was that this fits her described phenotype better than diabetic neuropathy does. Metformin and any PPI stack onto the same risk.

So: add TPO antibodies, intrinsic-factor and parietal-cell antibodies, and coeliac serology to the blood panel. Cheap, fast, and potentially the answer.

Would correcting thyroid status restore her sensation? Realistically, probably not much. If she is under-replaced, correction may improve energy, muscle power and cramps over 3–6 months. If she is over-replaced, dose reduction may meaningfully improve proximal strength and reduce her atrial fibrillation risk — which could matter more for “my legs won't hold me” than anything sensory. The point of checking is to remove a modifiable aggravator and avoid iatrogenic harm.

Section 10

Spine-specific analysis

This section carries the case's central unresolved disagreement, and it is worth understanding rather than papering over.

The mechanism, and the honest counter-argument

Standing increases the inward curve of the lower back. Extension narrows the spinal canal by several routes at once: the ligament buckles inward, discs bulge backward, and the facet joints override into the space where the nerve roots run. Two mechanisms are then proposed — venous congestion (the valveless epidural veins cannot drain past the compressed segment, causing swelling and impaired nutrition of the nerve roots) and arterial insufficiency (compression plus the metabolic demand of active roots outstrips their blood supply). Flexion reverses all of it. This is why the symptom is postural rather than activity-dependent, and why relief requires bending rather than merely stopping.

The debate, and where it landed

The neurologist initially argued no metabolic diagnosis can explain posture dependence. Challenged, it withdrew that claim as too strong — venous pooling and mechanical loading of the soles in an obese diabetic genuinely can aggravate nerves already at an ischaemic margin. But it rebutted the counter-claim that length-dependent neuropathy is “notoriously provoked by prolonged standing,” noting it could find no guideline or trial support for standing provocation as a recognised feature of diabetic neuropathy, which is classically worse at rest and at night.

Net result: stenosis contributing 45–55%; stenosis as the sole cause only 5–8%. The ascent above the knee almost certainly is not stenosis — a graded stocking migrating upward is dying-back nerve disease, whereas stenosis intensifies within fixed territories. And the disagreement is unresolved on current information because the brief only assumed that sitting relieves her. Hence the one question in section 04.

What to look for on imaging, and what to ignore

The single most important caveat in this dossier

Among asymptomatic 80-year-olds, roughly 96% have disc degeneration and 84% have disc bulges. Degenerative change at 80 is the default state of the human spine, not an explanation. A report reading “multilevel degenerative change with moderate canal narrowing” is the expected finding and means nothing on its own. Attribution requires the severity, level, side and posture-dependence to match the clinical syndrome. The specific error to guard against is anchoring on an incidental stenosis and never diagnosing the treatable cause.

Named lesions worth asking the radiologist to comment on specifically, because they are treatable and commonly under-reported: degenerative spondylolisthesis at L4/5 (strong predilection for elderly women, and only visible on standing films), epidural lipomatosis (obesity-related, symmetric, needs axial T1), synovial cyst, ossified ligament in the thoracic spine (devastating to miss; CT sees it better than MRI), and cord signal change with dorsal flow voids suggesting a dural AV fistula.

Treatment, and what surgery honestly delivers

Conservative care first is well supported and is the right starting point. A randomised trial found structured physiotherapy and surgical decompression produced no significant difference in symptoms or function at two years in patients willing to be randomised.

  • Epidural steroid injections: not supported. A 400-patient trial in the New England Journal of Medicine found steroid plus local anaesthetic gave minimal or no benefit over local anaesthetic alone at six weeks. In a diabetic, the steroid also causes days-to-weeks of raised blood sugar, and repeated injections cost bone density. Defensible as a single diagnostic trial — if a targeted injection abolishes her standing-provoked numbness, that localises the source. Not as a programme.
  • If surgery is considered: decompression alone, not fusion. Two 2016 trials disagreed, but a further randomised trial in 2021 found decompression alone non-inferior to decompression plus fusion even in degenerative spondylolisthesis. Fusion should be reserved for documented instability — a mobile slip on standing flexion–extension films — not the mere presence of a slip on MRI. Registry data in the over-80s are reassuring: more than 70% reach a meaningful improvement and severe complications are not increased, though 3-month complications rise to around 12%, mostly urinary infections.
  • Interspinous spacers: advise against. No difference in pain or disability versus decompression, with more reoperations.
  • The evidence is openly contradictory and should be presented that way. The SPORT trial favoured surgery through four years; a 2016 Cochrane review concluded “no clear benefit” with a 10–24% surgical side-effect rate against none reported for conservative care.
The answer the family will ask for

Decompression is an operation for walking distance and for leg pain. It is not an operation for numbness. Published follow-up shows numbness improves least of all symptoms, mostly in the first two weeks, and significantly less than pain does. Diabetes and a longer pre-operative symptom duration both predict persistent numbness — and she has both. The honest promise is: “you will probably stand and walk further before symptoms start, and any leg pain should settle; your feet will probably still feel numb.” Promising restored sensation would be dishonest, and dashed expectation is the commonest cause of dissatisfaction after this operation.

A treatment that doubles as a diagnostic test

Trial a four-wheeled rollator with a seat and formally record her walking distance and standing time before and after. A rollator induces exactly the forward trunk lean that decompresses a narrowed canal. If it markedly extends her range, that is real evidence for a postural spinal mechanism. If it helps only by providing support without changing her symptom-onset distance, the mechanism is probably not extension-dependent. It costs nothing and answers a question an MRI cannot.

Section 11

Vascular analysis

The vascular reviewer's verdict: peripheral arterial disease as the primary cause is about 3–5%; the probability it is present is about 30–40%. Both numbers matter, for different reasons.

Why it is not the explanation

Arterial claudication is an oxygen supply-and-demand problem in muscle. It requires work. Standing still barely raises calf oxygen demand, so arterial disease does not come on while standing motionless — and it is relieved by simply stopping, without needing to sit or bend. Her pattern is the opposite. Arterial disease also causes cramping pain, not numbness as the dominant complaint, and it is rarely so symmetric.

Why it must still be excluded

Because in a diabetic it is frequently silent: sensory and autonomic neuropathy blunts or abolishes the ischaemic pain that would normally warn her. Diabetic atherosclerosis also preferentially attacks the small vessels below the knee, so the pulses at the groin and behind the knee can feel normal while the foot is critically underperfused. In an insensate foot, unrecognised arterial disease is the direct pathway to ulceration, infection and amputation. Finding it also changes her cardiovascular management.

The test result that misleads

A normal ankle-brachial index does not exclude arterial disease in a diabetic. Calcification in the artery walls makes the vessels resist compression, so the cuff pressure needed to occlude them overestimates the true pressure — giving falsely normal or falsely high readings. One accuracy study found sensitivity of only about 35% in diabetes. An index of 1.1 in this patient is not reassurance.

Ask for the toe-brachial index — the digital arteries escape this calcification. Abnormal is 0.70 or below. European guidance specifically recommends it in diabetes and kidney disease, and in anyone with a normal resting ankle index but persisting suspicion.

Orthostatic and venous mechanisms — where vascular medicine genuinely does apply

Standing raises ankle venous pressure toward a hydrostatic column of 80–100 mmHg; obesity and a weak calf pump worsen it, and the resulting tissue swelling can compress small nerve fibres. But venous disease has a signature this case has not yet shown: worse at the end of the day, relieved by elevation, with varicosities and skin staining. Rated a contributor in perhaps 15–25%, mostly by adding swelling and discomfort.

Orthostatic hypotension is the important one. Defined as a sustained fall of 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic within three minutes of standing. It is near-inevitable in this patient: diabetic autonomic neuropathy impairs the reflex; GLP-1 therapy lowers blood pressure and causes mild volume depletion; and antihypertensive doses that were correct at her pre-weight-loss weight are now excessive. It will not explain a fixed ascending sensory deficit — but it is probably the dominant driver of her fall risk, and it is fixable.

Same-day vascular referral

Limb-threatening
  • Rest pain in the foot or toes without walking.
  • Night pain that wakes her and is relieved by hanging the foot out of bed — this is ischaemic rest pain.
  • Any ulcer, blister, black spot or non-healing wound — painless ones are more dangerous, not less.
  • Black tissue on any toe or the heel.
  • A suddenly cold, pale or mottled, pulseless, painful limb — emergency department now; irreversible muscle injury within hours.

Vascular risk management that applies regardless of the diagnosis

High-intensity statin therapy (and note: this is an argument for keeping the statin); single antiplatelet therapy if arterial disease is confirmed; blood pressure targeted to her standing reading, not just seated; relaxed, individualised glycaemic targets with hypoglycaemia avoided; smoking status established and cessation supported if relevant; and structured foot protection — podiatry referral, daily inspection including between the toes, never barefoot, no hot water bottles or radiators, professional nail care, properly fitted footwear, and a pre-agreed route to be seen within 24 hours if any new lesion appears. If arterial disease is confirmed, supervised exercise therapy is first-line with good evidence, though recumbent cycling or water-based work may be needed if her limitation turns out to be neurogenic.

Section 13

GLP-1 and weight-loss analysis

Six specific questions, answered directly.

Is the GLP-1 relevant to her neurological symptoms?

Indirectly, yes — in four ways, none of which is direct nerve toxicity: reduced intake risking B12, thiamine and protein inadequacy; lean-mass loss driving sarcopenia and instability; blood-pressure lowering plus volume depletion on unchanged antihypertensive doses causing orthostatic symptoms on standing; and, if it drove a fast HbA1c fall, the treatment-induced neuropathy route. Her symptoms began around a year ago and may predate the drug — the timeline must be established before any of this is attributed.

Do GLP-1s protect nerves in humans?

No — and this should not be implied

The animal and mechanistic data look supportive, but the one randomised human trial designed for this endpoint (exenatide versus insulin glargine, 46 patients, 18 months, matched HbA1c) found no significant difference in clinical neuropathy, nerve fibre density, autonomic measures, nerve conduction or quality of life. A recent meta-analysis reports improved nerve conduction velocity across heterogeneous studies — a surrogate endpoint, hypothesis-generating only. A GLP-1 cannot be prescribed as a neuropathy treatment, and her symptoms should not be expected to improve because of it. Both reviewers who examined this said so independently.

How much of the weight lost is muscle?

The direction is solid; the exact figure is contested. The tirzepatide DXA substudy showed roughly 26% of weight lost was lean tissue. The semaglutide figure is frequently quoted as ~40%, but one reviewer could trace that specific proportion only to secondary summaries and flagged it as unverified — so the honest range is roughly a quarter to two-fifths, with the precise number uncertain.

Why this matters far more at 80 than at 50: she starts from lower absolute muscle mass and poorer muscle quality, regains lean mass poorly after weight loss, and sits close to the thresholds at which strength loss becomes inability to rise from a chair or climb stairs. A 50-year-old absorbs a 5 kg lean loss; an 80-year-old may cross a disability threshold with it.

Distinguishing strength failure from sensory failure — and why it matters

Sensory failure: unsteadiness worse in the dark and with eyes closed; not knowing where the feet are. Strength failure: difficulty rising from a chair, climbing stairs, legs giving way after loading — which matches her standing- and walking-provoked pattern well. Both may coexist. The second one is genuinely reversible. If proximal power is reduced, the answer is not more neuropathy workup; it is protein and resistance training.

Could weight loss help?

Yes, genuinely — and this is an argument for continuing. Reduced spinal loading (directly relevant if canal narrowing is the postural driver), better mobility, less joint load, improved insulin sensitivity, improved sleep apnoea, better blood pressure and lipids. Central obesity and raised triglycerides are independent neuropathy risk factors in their own right. The tension is not “weight loss good versus bad” — it is fat loss (good) versus lean loss (bad), and the entire clinical task is to separate them.

Should the GLP-1 continue?

The endocrinology position — a prescriber conversation, not a unilateral change

Continue at the current dose, do not escalate further, and change the goal from weight loss to weight and body-composition maintenance — conditional on five things:

  1. The protein and resistance-training programme is actually in place. Non-negotiable precondition.
  2. Rate of loss no more than about 0.25–0.5 kg per week, with a floor weight agreed in advance.
  3. HbA1c not driven below roughly 7.0–7.5%, and no hypoglycaemia.
  4. No functional deterioration — grip strength, gait speed and sit-to-stand stable or improving.
  5. Adequate intake tolerated; no persistent nausea or dehydration.

Grounds to consider dose reduction or pause: an HbA1c fall over 2 points in 3 months with matching neurological worsening; falling grip strength or gait speed; new falls; continued loss below the agreed floor; persistent nausea with inadequate protein; kidney injury; new orthostatic hypotension. That she is still actively losing weight at 80 while developing a new functional deficit is itself a reason to consider reducing rather than escalating.

Is the weight-loss goal still appropriate at 80?

Not as an open-ended goal. The obesity paradox in the very elderly is real: overweight and mild obesity are associated with lower mortality than normal weight in older adults, and optimal BMI appears to rise with age. Weight-loss trajectories in older people — including intentional ones — associate with higher mortality, largely mediated by lean mass and frailty. ESPEN geriatric guidance is that weight-reducing diets should be considered only in obese older people with weight-related health problems, and only combined with exercise. She qualifies, so treatment is defensible — but the appropriate target is function and body composition, not a BMI number: modest fat loss of 5–10%, lean mass and strength preserved, glycaemia and blood pressure improved, mobility improved.

Two practical interactions to raise

  • Levothyroxine: delayed gastric emptying alters its absorption, while weight loss lowers the dose requirement. Recheck TSH and free T4 after every escalation and every 5–10% of weight lost.
  • Sick-day rules: what to hold during vomiting or diarrhoea, and when to check kidney function. This is the cascade in section 07 and it is preventable.
Section 14

The examination to insist on — free, and currently missing

This is where the case is actually decided. None of it requires equipment beyond a reflex hammer, a tuning fork, a blood-pressure cuff and ten minutes. The single largest information gap in this whole case is that nobody has documented whether she has any true muscle weakness, as opposed to sensory loss.

  1. Muscle power, graded and written down

    Hip flexion, knee extension, ankle dorsiflexion and plantarflexion, great-toe extension, each graded 0–5. Plus heel-walking, toe-walking, and rising from a chair without using her arms. Sensory loss is managed medically. New bilateral weakness from a compressive lesion is a surgical emergency. Nothing else on this list changes the urgency as much.

  2. The reflex pattern — the highest-yield 60 seconds in the case

    Absent ankle and knee jerks with downgoing toes → the problem is in the nerves; look at the lumbar spine. Brisk knee jerks, or an upgoing toe, means a spinal cord component → scan the whole spine and check B12 and copper urgently. The combination of absent ankle jerks with brisk knee jerks is the classic signature of two lesions at once and is common in diabetes. Do not let absent ankle jerks reassure anyone out of a cord lesion.

  3. Vibration, joint-position sense and Romberg

    A markedly positive Romberg with vibration lost out of proportion to pinprick points to the dorsal columns — B12, copper, or cord. Have someone guarding her: this patient will fall.

  4. Map the upper border of numbness in centimetres, and note whether it is sharp or gradual

    A gradual fade supports neuropathy. A sharp, mappable line — especially onto the trunk — means the spinal cord and changes everything. Record it so progression becomes measurable.

  5. Lying and standing blood pressure and pulse at 1 and 3 minutes

    Five minutes lying first. Record the pulse at every point: a BP fall without a rise in pulse means autonomic failure or beta-blockade rather than simple dehydration. Because her trigger is prolonged standing, extend to 5–10 minutes if the 3-minute reading is normal. A seated clinic reading measures the one posture in which she has no symptoms. Three separate reviewers insisted on this independently.

  6. The post-exertional examination — rarely done, close to diagnostic

    Examine reflexes, power and sensation before and immediately after 2–5 minutes of corridor walking or standing in extension. A new reflex loss or new sensory deficit that appears only after provocation is close to diagnostic of dynamic nerve-root compromise. Ask for it by name.

  7. The cauda equina screen — mandatory at this visit

    Saddle and perineal sensation, voluntary anal tone, and a post-void bladder scan. Not optional: she has a bilateral, symmetric, progressive deficit that has objectively ascended.

  8. Provocation and relief testing

    Time how long she can stand still before symptoms start — that number is her best outcome measure. Sustained standing extension for 30–60 seconds. The bicycle-versus-treadmill comparison: if she can pedal leaning forward for 20 minutes but cannot stand for 5, that is strong evidence for a postural spinal mechanism.

  9. Feet, pulses and skin — shoes and socks off, both feet

    Femoral, popliteal, posterior tibial and dorsalis pedis pulses. Note that an absent dorsalis pedis can be normal, but an absent posterior tibial pulse is rare and meaningful. Plus temperature gradient, capillary refill, hair loss, dependent rubor, swelling, and inspection between every toe for callus, cracks or ulceration.

  10. Objective baselines, recorded today and repeated at 6 and 12 weeks

    Timed Up and Go (over 13.5 seconds signals elevated fall risk), 4-metre gait speed (under 0.8 m/s impaired), 30-second chair stands, grip strength (under 16 kg in women is low). These convert “she seems worse” into data a specialist must act on.

    Common with obesity and diabetes, independently associated with neuropathy, and worsens daytime instability.

Her posture dependence and rapid symmetric ascent are precisely such atypical features, so the guideline supports imaging and referral in her case rather than discouraging it. That is the argument to make.

Section 16

Treatments with the strongest evidence

The single most important finding in the evidence review

The best-evidenced intervention available to this patient is not a drug. Supervised exercise reduces the rate of falls by 23% (rate ratio 0.77, 95% CI 0.71–0.83) on high-certainty evidence from 59 trials and 12,981 people of mean age 76. Balance and functional exercise alone gives 24%; balance combined with resistance training gives 34% (rate ratio 0.66, 0.50–0.88). The Otago home programme shows a 32% reduction.

No medication, supplement or device in this dossier approaches that level of evidence. And it is aimed at the right target: her chief complaints — losing the ground under her feet, legs not holding her — are balance and strength outcomes, not pain outcomes. This should be positioned ahead of every drug, not after them.

2. Symptom control — with an important caveat

These drugs treat pain. Her main complaint is not pain.

The entire neuropathic-pain pharmacopoeia targets pain. None of it restores sensation, and adding a sedating drug to a patient whose problem is unsteadiness can make her worse. That deserves explicit discussion before any prescription. If she has little or no pain, the honest answer may be that none of these drugs is indicated at all.

The AAN's 2022 guideline found that the main drug classes all have comparable effect sizes — there is no evidence-based first choice, so the decision is driven by harms. The OPTION-DM head-to-head trial confirmed no winner between duloxetine, pregabalin and amitriptyline (between-pathway difference −0.1 on an 11-point scale), but found one genuinely actionable thing: adding a second drug beats staying on a failing single drug (further reduction of 1.0 versus 0.2 points).

Section 18

Overhyped, unsupported, or to avoid

This section exists to save money, time and hope. Several of these are sold specifically to people in her exact situation.

The confident negatives — moderate-certainty evidence of no benefit

These are not “unproven.” They have been tested properly and did not work.

  • Alpha-lipoic acid. The 2024 Cochrane review (3 RCTs, 816 patients) concluded it probably has little or no effect on symptoms at 6 or 24 months. The 4-year NATHAN 1 trial failed its primary endpoint. Additional caution here: it can lower blood glucose (hypoglycaemia risk on top of her diabetes medication), has been linked to a rare autoimmune hypoglycaemia syndrome, and may interfere with levothyroxine absorption.
  • Benfotiamine at 12 months — no significant effect on any endpoint.
  • Near-infrared / photobiomodulation ("Anodyne", most "laser neuropathy" clinic packages). A double-blind sham-controlled trial found no difference in quality of life, balance or nerve conduction. Clinics charge substantially for courses of this.
  • Pulsed electromagnetic field therapy. A 225-patient sham-controlled trial was negative on both pain scales.
  • Epidural steroid injections for stenosis — see section 10.
Avoid — real harm potential
  • Proprietary "nerve support" / "nerve renew" blends. No trials of the actual formulations. They are the commonest route to B6 toxicity — which can cause or worsen exactly her symptoms. Many also contain high-dose biotin, which corrupts thyroid and cardiac blood tests in a woman on thyroid replacement with heart disease.
  • Any long-term vitamin B6 above about 10–25 mg/day. No benefit in her situation; dose-dependent and sometimes irreversible sensory neuropathy.
  • Stem cell and exosome clinics. No such product is approved for neuropathy anywhere. The FDA maintains a standing consumer alert; documented harms across reported adverse events include blindness, tumours, bloodstream infections and spinal abscesses. Costs run to tens of thousands.
  • Unregulated peptides bought online (BPC-157, cerebrolysin, thymosin and similar). Zero adequate human evidence in this condition; unlicensed, unverified purity, contamination and mislabelling risk, injection-site infection in a diabetic, unknown interactions with her cardiac drugs.
  • Chelation therapy. No mechanism, no evidence — and EDTA chelation has caused fatal low calcium and kidney failure. Catastrophic in a possible-CKD 80-year-old.
  • IV vitamin infusions and "Myers cocktails". No benefit over oral for this indication; line infection and fluid load in a cardiac patient.
  • Hyperbaric oxygen for neuropathy. Legitimate only for selected non-healing diabetic foot ulcers. Expensive, and carries cardiac and hypoglycaemia risk during sessions.
  • NMN / nicotinamide riboside. Preclinical only; no completed human neuropathy trial.
  • Unsupervised ketogenic or aggressive low-carbohydrate diets. No neuropathy evidence, and three real dangers: hypoglycaemia if she is on a sulfonylurea or insulin; dehydration and kidney injury; inadequate protein accelerating her sarcopenia. And if she is on an SGLT2 inhibitor — likely given diabetes plus cardiovascular disease — carbohydrate restriction can precipitate euglycaemic ketoacidosis, which is life-threatening and easily missed because the glucose reading looks normal.
  • High-velocity spinal manipulation. Fracture and arterial risk in an 80-year-old with likely osteoporosis and vascular disease.
  • Hot water bottles, heat pads and hot foot soaks "for circulation". Serious burns on insensate feet — a common route to a diabetic foot catastrophe. Actively discourage.
  • Reflexively stopping her statin. The evidence does not support statins as a cause of sensory neuropathy, and stopping removes proven protection in a high-risk 80-year-old.

Section 21

Three-month rehabilitation strategy

Physiotherapist-led, twice weekly for weeks 1–6 then weekly, with a daily home programme. All balance work with a stable support within reach and, initially, a second person present. The targets are standing tolerance, walking distance, strength and stability — not a lower pain score.

Weeks 1–4 — foundation and baseline

  • Flexion-biased decompression exercises, twice daily, plus as a “rescue” whenever symptoms start while standing: double-knee-to-chest hold 20–30 s ×5; single-knee-to-chest each side; supine pelvic tilt 10 s ×10; seated forward lean over the knees 30 s ×5; standing counter-lean with hands on a worktop and the lower back rounded, 30 s ×5.
  • Hip flexor and hamstring stretches 30 s ×3 each side daily — tight hip flexors force the arch that narrows the canal.
  • Core work without extension: abdominal bracing, dead-bug heel slides, bridging. No prone extension, no “superman”, no back-extension machines.
  • Strength, seated or machine-based, spine-neutral, 2 sets of 10–12, three times weekly (bodyweight only for the first fortnight): sit-to-stand from a chair progressing to no hands; seated leg press or wall squat to 45°; leg extension; heel raises holding support; hip abduction; and — critical for foot clearance and tripping — resisted ankle dorsiflexion with a band, 2 ×15.
  • Aerobic: stationary cycling 10 building to 20 minutes, five days weekly, at conversational effort; aquatic walking twice weekly if a pool with rails is available. Both avoid the extension loading and the standing provocation.
  • Balance, firm floor only, eyes open, support within reach: feet-together stand 30 s ×3; semi-tandem 20 s ×3 each; weight shifts. No foam pads, no eyes-closed work, no single-leg stance in this phase.
  • Record baselines at week 0: time standing still to symptom onset, distance to symptom onset, 4-metre gait speed, Timed Up and Go, 30-second chair stands.

Weeks 5–8 — loading and walking volume

  • Progress strength to 2–3 sets of 12–15 with light external load. Advance only when she completes the top of the range with good form and no symptom increase lasting beyond two hours.
  • Interval walking — the key strategy. Walk to about 70–80% of her symptom-onset distance, then sit or lean forward for 60–90 seconds until it clears, then resume. Four to six bouts, five days weekly. Total accumulated walking time is the outcome, not continuous distance — never walk into severe symptoms.
  • Add rollator- or pole-assisted walking for longer continuous distances.
  • Balance: tandem stance and tandem walking along a counter, step-ups to 10 cm, sideways and backwards walking with support, head turns while walking.

Weeks 9–12 — function and consolidation

  • Strength to 3 sets with added resistance; sit-to-stand from progressively lower seats; step-ups to 15–20 cm.
  • Aerobic to 25–30 minutes, five days weekly.
  • Rehearse the real tasks she has lost: a shop aisle, standing at the kitchen counter, getting up from the floor. Teach the footstool trick explicitly — standing at a counter with one foot on a low stool flexes the lower spine and often dramatically extends standing tolerance.
  • Dual-task walking (carrying something, counting) and turning drills.
  • Reassess at week 12 against every week-0 baseline. If standing tolerance and walking distance have not improved, escalate to a spinal surgical opinion.
Safety rules for someone who cannot feel her feet
  • Never barefoot or in socks alone. Never eyes-closed balance work at home. No foam or compliant surfaces unsupervised.
  • Daily foot inspection including between the toes. No heat sources. Professional nail and callus care only.
  • Good lighting everywhere — she is substituting vision for proprioception, so darkness is her highest-risk condition.
  • Occupational therapy home assessment: grab rails by the toilet and shower, non-slip bath mat, raised toilet seat, perching stool, handrails on both sides of stairs, frequently used items between waist and shoulder height, a personal alarm, and a written falls plan for the family.

  18. “What specifically should make us call you or go to hospital?” Get it written down.

Every conclusion in this dossier is provisional on these. They are ranked by how much they would change the analysis.

  1. Is there any objective muscle weakness?

    MRC-graded power, heel and toe walking, chair rise. Determines whether this is a medical or a surgical problem, and sets the urgency. The largest single gap.

  2. The reflex pattern and plantar responses

    Decides nerve versus spinal cord, and therefore what gets scanned.

    Resolves the central disagreement between the neurology and spine reviewers. Free to obtain.

  5. Pain versus pure numbness

    Burning pain redirects toward small-fibre disease, amyloid and treatment-induced neuropathy. Painless large-fibre loss redirects toward B12, dorsal columns and CIDP. It also determines whether any pain drug is indicated at all.

    The reversible causes.

    Tests treatment-induced neuropathy from existing records.

    Tests whether diabetes is the culprit at all.

    Short duration weakens the diabetic attribution; Hashimoto's raises the B12 pathway sharply.

The honest summary for the family

Her numbness will most likely not be reversed by any supplement. But three things on this list could genuinely change her life: finding a reversible cause (B12 deficiency, B6 toxicity, or treatable spinal narrowing), a supervised balance-and-strength programme, and a proper medication review. The thing most likely to end her independence is not the numbness — it is a fall and a hip fracture. That risk is measurably reducible starting this week, before any diagnosis is made.

Those are achievable targets, and progress against them can be measured — which is why the baselines in section 14 matter more than they look.

Claims the reviewers explicitly could not support
  • No guideline or trial evidence was found establishing prolonged standing as a recognised provoking factor for length-dependent diabetic polyneuropathy — the crux of the central disagreement in section 10.
  • No trial or guideline on GLP-1 receptor agonist effects on diabetic neuropathy progression, beyond the single null randomised study cited.
  • No FDA or EMA safety communication addresses neurological or nutritional harm from GLP-1 agonists. What does exist is a class-wide label change for pulmonary aspiration under anaesthesia, and a European addition of a very rare optic neuropathy to semaglutide labelling — optic, not peripheral, and it should not be conflated with this presentation.
  • “Double crush” combining polyneuropathy and lumbar stenosis is mechanistic reasoning, not an evidence-based entity.
  • Direct evidence that weight loss improves stenosis symptoms specifically; benefit of textured insoles; and supporting trials for pulsed electromagnetic field therapy or hyperbaric oxygen in neuropathy — none confirmed.

How this was produced. Seven specialist reviews — neurology and geriatric internal medicine, evidence synthesis, clinical pharmacology, spine and rehabilitation, vascular medicine, endocrinology, and integrative/emerging therapies — were conducted independently, then cross-examined against one another. The neurology review materially revised its conclusions in response to the spine review's challenge: the combined neuropathy-plus-stenosis construct was downgraded from very high to moderate likelihood, vitamin B6 toxicity was upgraded from low to moderate, and its single top recommendation changed from “MRI lumbar spine” to the free bedside examination and supplement audit. Where reviewers disagreed and the evidence could not settle it, both positions are reported.
